# Hearthwood

A cozy medieval woodland game. Choose a frog, a fox or a bear, walk out of a
small village into an enormous wood, and look at the ground.

Every fallen stick in Hearthwood is a different stick. Bring the interesting
ones back to Old Nissel the Stickwright and she will make them into something.

**No asset files.** There are no models, textures or sounds in this repository.
Every tree, house, animal, mushroom, stick and note of music is generated by
code at runtime. The whole game is one folder of text plus a copy of three.js.

The day runs on a sixteen-minute cycle. It starts in the middle of a golden
afternoon; stay out long enough and the lanterns come on, the windows glow and
the fireflies come up out of the wet ground.

## The core loop

1. Walk out of the village. The fences are about a hundred and eighty metres
   from the green in every direction; past them is nine hundred metres of wood
   in every direction after that.
2. Look down. Sticks lie where wood actually falls — thickest under old trees,
   round fallen logs, thin in the meadows, absent on the swept village paths.
3. Pick up the interesting ones. Length, thickness, curvature, forks, moss,
   fungi, bark, breakage and ten rare forms are all rolled per stick, and the
   rare forms are silhouettes you can spot from thirty metres.
4. Bring them to the Stickwright. Twenty-one recipes, each written as a
   requirement on a piece of wood — "a long straight limb", "a burl-headed
   limb", "a besom-bunch and something with foxfire in it".
5. The weapon is built FROM THE STICK YOU BROUGHT, by the same generator, with
   the same seed. Your sword has your branch's knots in it.

## The numbers that matter

| | |
|---|---|
| map | 1800 × 1800 m |
| village | π·176² m² = **3.00%** of the map |
| village buildings | 37–38, laid out along a street and four lanes |
| named villagers | 23, plus chickens, cats and ducks |
| tree species | 11, each with its own posture and silhouette |
| stick body plans | 7 common, 10 rare forms |
| weapon recipes | 21, across 12 classes |
| save size | ~32 KB (seeds and specs, never geometry) |

`villageFraction()` in `src/core/Config.js` computes the 3% rather than
asserting it, and `tools/test_world.mjs` fails if it drifts.

### The load-bearing ideas

- **`MeshBuilder` (art/Geo.js)** — four attributes: position, normal, colour
  and **sway**. Everything in the world is triangles written into one of
  these, and whole tiles of forest are merged so a thousand unique plants cost
  one draw call.
- **Colour is linear.** `MeshBuilder.color()` converts sRGB hex to linear on
  the way into the buffer, because three.js treats a `color` attribute as
  already being in the working space. Skipping that step makes every mid-tone
  roughly three times too bright, which is exactly what it did the first time.
- **Wind lives in the material**, driven by the per-vertex `sway` weight and a
  phase taken from world position — so a gust travels across the wood instead
  of every plant nodding in unison.
- **`Terrain` is the single source of truth** about where the ground is, what
  it is made of, how wet it is, how dark it is and what grows there. Nothing
  else computes any of it.
- **The streamer builds tiles in three parts** (ground, bulk, floor) because
  the budget can only stop *between* jobs, and a whole detail-0 tile is a
  tenth of a second — a hitch you can feel.
- **The near grid is a whole number of far blocks.** A far block is exactly
  2×2 near tiles and the detailed ring is the 3×3 block neighbourhood, so the
  two resolutions tile each other perfectly: no overlap to z-fight and no gap
  to see through.
- **No custom post-processing pass.** ACES and the sRGB encode are three.js's
  own; the vignette is CSS. A hand-written composite shader that forgets its
  sRGB encode makes the whole game a gamma too dark, invisibly.

## Working on it

There is no npm here. The suites and render scripts run under VS Code's
Electron as Node:

```powershell
.\tools\run.ps1 test_world.mjs   <outDir>   # terrain, village, scatter, sticks
.\tools\run.ps1 test_game.mjs    <outDir>   # the whole game, headless, 45 s walk
.\tools\run.ps1 render_sticks.mjs  <outDir> [sheet|rare|<form>]
.\tools\run.ps1 render_trees.mjs   <outDir> [sheet|lod|<species>]
.\tools\run.ps1 render_plants.mjs  <outDir>
.\tools\run.ps1 render_animals.mjs <outDir>
.\tools\run.ps1 render_weapons.mjs <outDir>
.\tools\run.ps1 render_scene.mjs   <outDir> [village|street|forest|aerial|river|all]
```

`tools/rasterlib.mjs` is a software rasteriser that writes PNGs through the
**same** tone map and sRGB encode as the game. It has caught more real bugs
than every assertion put together: a village in a green void, ferns that were
grass, a boulder inside the Stickwright's workshop, eyes that were bumps. A
render only proves something if it goes through the same pipeline as the game.

**Look at the art before believing it is fine.**

## Not in this build, on purpose

No combat, no quests, no farming, no fishing, no crafting trees, no
progression curve. The brief was to build one loop properly rather than ten
loops badly. What is here is meant to be finished, not to be a foundation with
scaffolding still on it.
